AI-Based Medical Decision-Support System

Cross-modal fusion of clinical symptoms and chest radiographs through a shared diagnostic vocabulary

Group: RG24  ·  Course: Major Project-I (18B19CI791)
Supervisor: Dr. Hari Singh
Report date: 13 November 2026  ·  Status: Experimental evaluation complete

Summary. The three proposed datasets contained no overlapping patients, so multimodal fusion had no paired data to learn from. We resolved this by identifying the symptom dataset as DDXPlus (610,000 patients, 223 standardised clinical questions) and parsing the free-text indication field of each Open-I chest X-ray report into that same 223-code vocabulary, yielding 3,459 genuinely paired patients.

Four models were trained on an identical patient split and evaluated on eight thoracic findings. Cross-modal fusion attains macro AUC 0.842 against 0.822 for the image-only baseline and 0.622 for the clinical-only baseline, ranking first on four of the eight findings: the central hypothesis is supported. Unexpectedly, the best fusion configuration is the one without DDXPlus pre-training; transferring the pre-trained encoder weights reduced performance to 0.808. We attribute this to a measured distribution mismatch — DDXPlus questionnaires are dense, whereas Open-I indications yield a mean of 1.35 symptoms per patient, with 8.3% irrecoverably de-identified at source. Both the positive result and this limitation are reported in full.

1. Problem addressed

The project's central premise — that symptoms and radiographs can be fused to improve diagnosis — required paired data that the proposed datasets did not provide.

The symptom questionnaire, chest X-ray, and diabetes datasets originate from three disjoint patient populations. No patient appears in more than one. Consequently, symptom features showed no correlation with radiographic findings: there was no mechanism linking any patient's reported symptoms to their radiograph, and the fusion architecture had nothing to fuse. This was a data-pairing problem, not a modelling deficiency.

2. Method: a shared clinical vocabulary

2.1 Dataset identification

The symptom dataset was identified as the DDXPlus research corpus rather than the 41-disease dataset originally assumed: 610,000 patients, 223 standardised binary clinical questions (symptoms and medical history), and 49 diagnoses, including Pneumonia, Bronchitis, URTI, Influenza, Tuberculosis and COPD exacerbation.

2.2 Recovering the cross-modal link

Each Open-I radiology report contains an indication field — the referring physician's stated reason for the examination, in free text:

"68-year-old with shortness of breath and weakness. Hypertension. Diabetes."

We parse this text into the same 223 DDXPlus question codes using a curated rule set. Every radiograph therefore acquires a symptom vector belonging to the same patient, producing the paired multimodal data the project requires. Because both modalities share one vocabulary, a symptom encoder pre-trained on 610,000 DDXPlus patients can be transferred to the much smaller Open-I corpus — the principal technical contribution of this work.

3. System architecture

Symptoms (223 standard questions) Chest radiograph (frontal) │ │ SymptomEncoder (pre-trained DenseNet-121 on 610k DDXPlus patients) (ImageNet init, 7×7×1024) │ │ └──► bidirectional cross-modal attention ◄──┘ │ 8 thoracic findings (multi-label): normal · chest infection · cardiomegaly · pleural effusion opacity · atelectasis · emphysema/COPD · pulmonary edema │ Vitals ──► Diabetes model ──► TRIAGE ENGINE ──► Grad-CAM explanation │ LOW / MODERATE / HIGH / EMERGENCY + referral

The triage engine encodes the comorbidity interaction that motivated the project: a suspected chest infection in a patient with elevated diabetes risk is escalated above either finding in isolation, since impaired immune response worsens pneumonia outcomes.

4. Experimental design

Four models were trained on an identical patient-level split (2,421 train / 519 validation / 519 test), stratified on the chest-infection label, so that all comparisons are direct:

  • Baseline A — image only: DenseNet-121 on the radiograph alone.
  • Baseline B — clinical only: SymptomEncoder on the parsed symptom vector alone.
  • Proposed — cross-modal fusion: both modalities with bidirectional attention.
  • Ablation — fusion without DDXPlus pre-training: isolates the contribution of the shared vocabulary.

Principal result. The cross-modal fusion model attains macro AUC 0.8416 against 0.8216 for the image-only baseline (+0.020) and 0.6221 for the clinical-only baseline. Fusion ranks first on four of the eight findings. The project's central hypothesis — that combining symptoms with the radiograph improves prediction over either modality alone — is therefore supported on this corpus.

An unexpected finding, reported in full. The best fusion configuration is the one without DDXPlus pre-training. Transferring the pre-trained encoder weights reduced performance to 0.8084 — below even the image-only baseline. The probable cause is a distribution mismatch: the encoder was pre-trained on DDXPlus questionnaires, in which each patient has many recorded symptoms, whereas the Open-I indications yield a mean of 1.35. A representation optimised for dense input carries an unhelpful prior on sparse input.

The shared vocabulary itself remains necessary — without it no paired dataset exists and no fusion model could be trained. It is specifically the weight transfer that did not help here. This distinction was detected only because the ablation was run, which is its purpose.

6.1 Source-level de-identification

The Open-I corpus was de-identified before publication, with clinical terms replaced by the token XXXX. A substantial fraction of indications therefore carry no recoverable content — for example "XXXX-year-old XXXX with XXXX." No parser improvement can recover these; we quantify them at 8.3% of all patients (67 blank, 191 fully redacted).

Two of the four most frequent features are patient sex, which carries almost no information about thoracic findings. The clinically informative features appear in fewer than 4% of patients each. The branch's most available signal is thus its least diagnostic. This bounds the clinical baseline at AUC 0.622 and explains why fusion's advantage over the image branch, while consistent, is modest (+0.020): the second modality is thin. It also explains the pre-training result — an encoder fitted to dense DDXPlus questionnaires transfers a prior that does not hold at 1.35 symptoms per patient.

6.3 Reconciliation with the earlier lift analysis

This does not contradict the reported symptom–finding lifts. The lift figures (e.g. hypoxia → chest infection, 5.2×) are computed on the subsets where each symptom is present — 33 patients for hypoxia, 30 for heart disease. A feature may be strongly predictive when present while remaining uninformative to a classifier that observes it in roughly 1% of cases. Both measurements are correct; they answer different questions.

6.4 Parser improvement and its measured ceiling

We audited the 296 indications that parsed to an empty vector despite containing clinical words, and extended the rule set accordingly (rales/crackles, elevated blood pressure, syncopal episode, pulmonary nodule, upper respiratory tract infection, throat pain, chronic airway obstruction, headache, weight loss).

A design decision is worth recording: DDXPlus E_53 ("pain somewhere, related to your reason for consulting") matches the 52 cases of site-less or redacted pain, but applying it to every mention of pain tagged 30.9% of patients, making it the corpus's most frequent feature while duplicating the site-specific pain codes. It was therefore implemented as a fallback, firing only where no specific pain site matched (final prevalence 4.1%).

Measured effect. 87 of 296 cases recovered; symptom density 1.28 → 1.35 per patient; empty vectors 17.8% → 15.2%; paired patients 3,105 → 3,459 (+11%). Baseline B improved from AUC 0.581 to 0.622. The improvement is real but bounded: it does not close the gap to the image branch, consistent with the sparsity analysis above.

Three defects were identified and corrected during experimentation; two would have produced silently invalid results.

The encoder fix remaps weights by symptom name using the feature list stored in the checkpoint; all 231 shared symptoms were verified to transfer to their correct index, with the two new codes retaining fresh initialisation.

8. Limitations

  • Test set size and statistical confidence. 519 patients. Rare findings are sparsely represented (pulmonary edema: 17 cases; chest infection: 27), so per-class figures for these labels are noisy. The reported +0.020 macro-AUC advantage is from a single split and has not been subjected to a significance test; it should be read as a consistent trend across four of eight findings rather than as an established effect size.
  • DDXPlus is synthetic. The 99.34% symptom-model accuracy reflects a dataset generated from a clinical knowledge base; it demonstrates pipeline correctness, not clinical performance. The honest evaluation is the Open-I result.
  • Indication text is a proxy. It records the referring physician's stated reason for the examination, which is not a complete symptom history and is written by a clinician who already suspected a particular finding.
  • Not clinically validated. This is a research prototype for decision support. It is not a diagnostic device and requires qualified medical review.

9. Conclusions and proposed next steps

The shared-vocabulary method successfully produced paired multimodal data from datasets that previously had no patient overlap, and the full pipeline — symptom model, diabetes model, fusion architecture, Grad-CAM explainability, triage engine and demonstration application — is implemented and verified. On this corpus, cross-modal fusion improves on the image-only baseline (macro AUC 0.842 vs 0.822, best on four of eight findings), supporting the project's central hypothesis. The gain is modest, and transferring pre-trained encoder weights proved counter-productive; both observations are attributable to quantified sparsity in the paired symptom signal.

  1. Evaluate on MIMIC-CXR, which pairs full radiology reports with structured clinical data under less aggressive de-identification. This is the highest-value next step.
  2. Restrict fusion to cardiac findings, where the clinical branch carries genuine signal (cardiomegaly AUC 0.700; pulmonary edema 0.743) as opposed to parenchymal findings (opacity 0.528). This is clinically coherent: a referral citing heart failure describes a cardiac syndrome, whereas opacity has no distinctive presenting symptom.
  3. Revise the pre-training strategy. Direct weight transfer reduced performance. Two principled alternatives: fine-tune the encoder on Open-I's sparsity rather than freezing the transferred prior, or pre-train on artificially sparsified DDXPlus samples so that the source distribution matches the target.
  4. Establish statistical confidence. The +0.020 margin is measured on a single split. Repeating all four models across multiple seeds and reporting mean ± standard deviation, with a DeLong test on the paired AUCs, would substantiate the claim at low cost. We regard this as the most important immediate step.
  5. Regularise the fusion model. The pre-trained variant early-stopped at epoch 10, indicating overfitting with 2,421 training cases against 9M parameters; freezing the image backbone and tuning the auxiliary loss weight are the natural interventions.
